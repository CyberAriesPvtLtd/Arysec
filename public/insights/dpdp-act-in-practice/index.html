The DPDP Act in Practice: What Indian Businesses Must Do Now

India's DPDP Act 2023 is now backed by notified Rules and a commencement timetable. A practitioner's read on notice, consent, data principal rights, breach intimation, Significant Data Fiduciary duties and the readiness work that has to happen first.

Key takeaways

  • Core DPDP obligations commence 13 May 2027; the Consent Manager registration framework starts a year earlier, on 13 November 2026.
  • Consent must be free, specific, informed and unambiguous, and the itemised notice behind it is where most Indian privacy notices fail.
  • Breach intimation carries no harm threshold, and its seventy-two hour report clock runs alongside the CERT-In six-hour reporting duty.
  • Start with data discovery and processing records; notice rewrites and rights workflows built on guesswork will not survive an inquiry.

Where the law stands and who it reaches

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 received assent in August 2023 but waited two years for its operating machinery. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025, with staged commencement: Board provisions at once, Consent Manager registration from 13 November 2026, and the substantive obligations covering notice, consent, security safeguards, breach intimation, retention, rights and the Significant Data Fiduciary regime from 13 May 2027.

A Data Fiduciary determines the purpose and means of processing personal data. A Data Principal is the individual it relates to. A Data Processor processes on a fiduciary's behalf. Nearly every duty sits with the Fiduciary, which stays answerable for processing done for it, while processors carry almost no direct statutory obligation. Contract is your only instrument over a processor.

The Act covers digital personal data collected digitally or digitised later, and reaches processing abroad tied to offering goods or services to Data Principals in India. Processing by an individual for a purely personal or domestic purpose sits outside it, as does personal data the Data Principal has made publicly available themselves. There is no special category tier: health records and biometrics attract the same treatment as a delivery address, which pushes risk towards volume and context rather than data type.

Notice and consent carry the weight

Processing rests on consent or on defined legitimate uses such as employment purposes, legal compliance and medical emergency. There is no balancing test equivalent to legitimate interests under the EU GDPR, so anything outside that list needs consent: free, specific, informed, unconditional, unambiguous, and given by clear affirmative action. Bundled consent and pre-ticked boxes fail that test.

Each consent request must be preceded or accompanied by a notice whose contents the Rules prescribe. It must stand alone and be available in English or any Eighth Schedule language at the Data Principal's option, which alone defeats most privacy notices published in India.

The Consent Manager is a further creation of the Act: an entity registered with the Board through which a Data Principal gives, reviews and withdraws consent across several fiduciaries. Registration conditions are demanding, including incorporation in India, net worth of at least two crore rupees, and keeping routed personal data unreadable to itself.

  • An itemised description of the personal data collected, not a category label
  • The specified purpose and the services it enables
  • How to withdraw consent, made as easy as giving it
  • Where to exercise rights and raise a grievance with you
  • A means of complaining to the Data Protection Board of India
  • Working links or contacts for each of those actions

The rights you have to be able to service

Rights are fewer than under the GDPR, and each is an operational commitment rather than a policy line. There is no portability right and no general right to object. What exists has to work on demand, at whatever volume your users generate.

Two catch organisations out. Access requires you to name downstream recipients, so an accurate vendor and integration inventory stops being optional. Nomination has no analogue in most privacy programmes: no field for a nominee, no verification path for an incapacity claim, no runbook for the person who arrives holding one.

  • Access: a summary of personal data processed and of processing activities, plus who it was shared with and what was shared
  • Correction, completion and updating of inaccurate, incomplete or misleading data
  • Erasure on request, unless retention is necessary for the purpose or required by law
  • Grievance redressal through a readily available mechanism, capped by the Rules at ninety days
  • Nomination of another individual to exercise the rights on death or incapacity

Children's data and verifiable parental consent

A child is anyone under eighteen. Processing a child's personal data, or that of a person with a disability who has a lawful guardian, requires verifiable parental or guardian consent. The Rules point towards due diligence against reliable identity details you already hold, or a virtual token mapped to a verified identity issued by an entity entrusted by law or a digital locker provider.

Two prohibitions sit alongside it: no processing likely to harm a child's well-being, and no tracking, behavioural monitoring or targeted advertising directed at children. Certain classes of fiduciary and purpose, including some healthcare and education, are exempted from parts of this. If children can plausibly reach your product, treat age assurance as a design problem now.

The Significant Data Fiduciary tier

You do not self-designate as a Significant Data Fiduciary. The Central Government notifies an organisation or a class, weighing volume and sensitivity of data processed, risk to Data Principals, impact on India's sovereignty and integrity, electoral democracy, security of the State and public order. Because the trigger is a notification rather than a threshold you can calculate, you cannot rule the tier out yourself.

The Data Protection Officer requirement rewards close reading: a named individual based in India, with a reporting line to the board and published contact details, not a shared inbox and not a group privacy officer abroad.

  • Appoint a Data Protection Officer based in India, answerable to the governing body and named for grievance redressal
  • Engage an independent data auditor to evaluate compliance with the Act and Rules
  • Carry out a Data Protection Impact Assessment and audit every twelve months, reporting significant observations to the Board
  • Verify that algorithmic software handling personal data does not risk the rights of Data Principals
  • Observe restrictions on transferring specified categories of personal data outside India

Breach intimation without a harm threshold

A personal data breach means any unauthorised processing of personal data, or accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, use, alteration, destruction or loss of access, that compromises its confidentiality, integrity or availability. There is no risk threshold of the kind Article 33 of the GDPR provides, so the duty to intimate the Board and every affected Data Principal is not filtered by a likelihood-of-harm test. A misconfigured storage bucket holding a thousand email addresses is reportable on the face of it.

The Rules set a two-stage clock. Intimate the Board without delay with the nature, extent, timing, location and likely impact, then file a detailed report within seventy-two hours covering circumstances, mitigation, who caused it and remedial steps. Affected individuals get a plain description, the likely consequences, what you have done and who to contact. The clock starts on awareness, not on conclusion of the investigation.

This runs in parallel with the CERT-In directions of April 2022, requiring certain cyber incidents to be reported within six hours of noticing, and with sectoral duties from the Reserve Bank of India and SEBI. Your runbook needs one severity assessment that fires all of them, since the six-hour window is usually the binding constraint.

Transfers, retention and what failure costs

The Act takes a permissive default on cross-border transfer. Section 16 allows processing outside India except in countries the Central Government restricts by notification, a negative list rather than an adequacy regime. The qualification matters more than the rule: nothing there displaces a law providing higher protection, so Reserve Bank of India payment data localisation and sectoral requirements still bind you.

Retention is purpose-bound for most organisations. Erase when consent is withdrawn or as soon as it is reasonable to assume the purpose is no longer served, whichever is earlier, and make your processors erase too. Three classes of large platform get a fixed cap instead: e-commerce and social media intermediaries above two crore users, and online gaming intermediaries above fifty lakh, must erase three years after last engagement, with advance notice.

Penalties are financial rather than criminal, and graded by failure. Inadequate security safeguards carry the highest ceiling, running to hundreds of crores of rupees, followed by failure to intimate a breach and by children's data breaches, with a lower ceiling for Significant Data Fiduciary duties and a residuary category for everything else. The Board sets quantum by gravity, duration, repetition, mitigation and proportionality, which is why documenting your effort matters even where you fall short.

A readiness sequence that survives contact

The sequence below is ordered by dependency, not ease. Rewriting notices before you know what you hold is how these programmes stall, because you cannot describe processing you cannot enumerate.

Test the rights process with real requests against real accounts before commencement, timing each step. Keep evidence as you go, because when the Board weighs proportionality your contemporaneous records are the only version of events that carries weight.

  • Data discovery across production systems, warehouses, analytics tools, SaaS platforms, shared drives and backups, with owner interviews closing what scanning misses
  • Records of processing: purpose, ground, categories, retention, recipients and transfer route per activity, the artefact everything downstream rests on
  • Notice and consent redesign against the itemised requirement, with language options, easy withdrawal, and consent records carrying timestamps and versions
  • Rights handling: identity verification, cross-system search, exemption analysis, templates, a case log and a published response period you can meet
  • Vendor contracts, since processors owe you nothing directly: safeguards, breach timelines inside yours, deletion on instruction and audit rights
  • Breach runbook with one severity assessment triggering DPDP, CERT-In and sectoral notifications together, plus pre-approved drafts and a named decision maker
