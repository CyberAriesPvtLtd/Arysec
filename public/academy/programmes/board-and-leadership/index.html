Leadership & Response

Oversight You Can Exercise Without Being Technical

Boards are accountable for cyber risk and are usually briefed in a language designed to prevent follow-up questions. This session gives directors the small set of questions that reveal whether a security programme is real, and the judgement to read the answers.

The Board's Job Is Oversight, Not Operation

Directors are not expected to configure a firewall. They are expected to satisfy themselves that management has identified the risks that could materially damage the business, has a plan proportionate to those risks, and is reporting honestly on progress. Doing that requires a handful of good questions rather than technical fluency.

Most board security papers are written to reassure. A green dashboard, a patching percentage and a mention of an upcoming certification can all be true while the organisation remains one compromised mailbox away from a very bad quarter. We show what those reports leave out and what to ask instead.

The session is deliberately concrete: real incidents, what they cost, what the board knew beforehand, and the decision points that arrived faster than anyone expected. It ends with the decisions worth taking in advance, so they are not being made for the first time during an incident.

What the Board Leaves With

The intended result is a shorter, sharper security item on the agenda, with a reporting pack the board can actually interrogate.

  • A standing question set for management on cyber risk
  • A view of what a useful board security report contains
  • Agreed risk appetite statements for the risks that could stop the business
  • Clarity on who declares an incident and who authorises emergency spend
  • A position taken in advance on ransom payment and disclosure
  • An understanding of DPDP, CERT-In and sector reporting obligations
  • A defined cadence for reporting, exercising and independent assurance

Content

Session Content

What Actually Causes Losses

Business email compromise, ransomware and third-party failure, with real figures. Where the money goes: response, downtime, legal, remediation and lost contracts.

Reading a Risk Register

Telling a live register from a decorative one. Ownership, treatment dates that keep moving, and the accepted risks that were never actually accepted by anyone with the authority.

Questions to Ask Management

A short set that reliably distinguishes a real programme from a reported one: what would stop us trading, what did the last test find, when was the plan exercised, who is not doing what they said.

Regulatory and Legal Exposure

DPDP duties and intimation, CERT-In directions and timelines, sector regulators, contractual notification obligations, and where director duties actually bite.

Decisions Before the Incident

Ransom position, disclosure thresholds, delegated spend authority, and who speaks publicly — settled calmly rather than at two in the morning.

Assurance and Investment

What certification does and does not prove, when independent testing is worth buying, and how to judge whether a security budget request is proportionate.

How It Runs

From Scoping to Delivery

How a Board & Leadership engagement runs, start to finish.

01

Pre-Session Review

We read the last few board security papers and the risk register under NDA, so the session addresses your actual reporting rather than a generic example.

02

The Briefing

Half a day, or two 90-minute sessions if diaries demand it. Discussion-led, with a short scenario walkthrough to surface where accountability is unclear.

03

Agreeing Positions

The session ends by writing down the decisions taken — appetite, thresholds, authority — so they exist as a record rather than as a recollection.

04

Reporting Template

A board reporting template that fits on two pages and can be challenged, handed over for management to adopt.

Benefits

What Changes Afterwards

Better Questions, Better Answers

Management responds to what it is asked. Boards that ask about exercised plans and open findings get programmes built around those things.

Faster Decisions Under Pressure

Pre-agreed thresholds and spending authority remove hours from the response, and those hours are the expensive ones.

Demonstrable Oversight

Regulators, insurers and acquirers all look for evidence that the board engaged. Minuted decisions and a reporting cadence are that evidence.

Proportionate Spending

Directors who can read a risk register stop approving tools and start approving outcomes, which is usually both cheaper and more effective.
