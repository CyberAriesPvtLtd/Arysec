What Managed Means Here

Working IT is invisible, which is why it is usually under-resourced until it fails. The common pattern in a growing organisation is one or two capable generalists, a folder of passwords, an asset list that stopped being accurate two hires ago, and a patching routine that depends on somebody remembering. Nothing looks wrong from the outside. Then a laptop goes missing with no record of whether it was encrypted, a leaver's mailbox is still forwarding six weeks after the exit interview, a renewal invoice arrives for forty seats when thirty-one people work here, and a firewall turns out to be running firmware three years old because the engineer who owned it moved on. None of these is dramatic on its own. Together they are how most avoidable incidents and most audit findings begin.

A managed service means we take operational ownership of a defined estate and run it to a written standard. That covers a service desk with tiered first, second and third line support and a priority matrix your staff can predict; managed agents pushing patches to operating systems and third-party software in staged rings; device provisioning through Windows Autopilot, Apple Business Manager or Android zero-touch enrolment, so a new joiner receives a machine already built, encrypted and enrolled in mobile device management; Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace tenant administration; and identity lifecycle work driven from an agreed HR trigger rather than an email to whoever is free. Endpoint baselines follow CIS Benchmarks, and the controls we operate map to CIS Controls v8 and NIST CSF 2.0, so the standard has a public reference point instead of resting on our judgement alone.

Two things decide whether an arrangement like this survives years rather than months. The first is that security is not sold back to you later as an upgrade. Patch latency, local administrator rights, multi-factor coverage, backup restore testing and how quickly a leaver loses access are ordinary IT tasks, and they are also the controls an ISO/IEC 27001:2022 or SOC 2 assessor asks about first, so we run them as scheduled work and evidence them every month. The second is commercial. A fixed monthly fee against a scope written into the agreement removes any incentive to bill hours and lets you plan a budget, and what falls outside that scope is stated at the start rather than discovered in an invoice. Monthly service reporting shows performance against the agreed targets, and a quarterly business review turns recurring faults, ageing hardware and your licence position into a plan you can fund.

Patching and Monitoring

Provisioning and Device Management

Microsoft 365 and Workspace

Identity and Access Lifecycle

Joiner, mover and leaver requests run from an agreed HR trigger: group-based entitlements, multi-factor enrolment, separated administrative accounts, same-day revocation on exit, and periodic access reviews you can hand to an auditor.

Assets, Licences and Vendors

One register holds every device, warranty date, software licence and subscription renewal. We reconcile assigned licences against active staff, and take vendor tickets with your ISP, hardware suppliers and software providers.

How It Works

Our Engagement Process

How a Managed IT engagement runs from first conversation to steady state.

01

Discover

We audit what you actually have: devices, users, tenants, licences, network kit, backups, contracts and administrator accounts. The output is a documented estate and a list of risks worth fixing before anything else.

02

Transition

Tooling and agents are deployed in waves, credentials reset into your own tenants, and runbooks written. Cutover from the incumbent is staged with a defined date and followed by a hypercare period.

03

Operate

Day to day the service desk takes tickets, patch rings run on schedule, monitoring raises problems before users do, and joiner and leaver requests are actioned against the agreed timetable.

04

Improve

Monthly reporting shows performance against service levels. Each quarter we sit down with the numbers, the recurring faults worth engineering out, the hardware nearing end of life, and next year's plan.

Managed IT — Common Questions

Usually not. Most engagements are co-managed: we take the repeatable, tooling-heavy work such as patching, monitoring, provisioning, tenant administration and first-line tickets, while your manager keeps what needs business context, including application decisions, supplier relationships, budget and internal projects. We agree a responsibility matrix at the start so nothing falls into the gap between us. Where an organisation has no internal IT at all, we provide the whole function and a named account lead.

Response is committed; resolution is targeted. A four-hour figure that promises resolution is not credible when the cause is a dead motherboard or an upstream ISP fault, so the agreement separates the two and sets resolution targets per priority. It also defines what qualifies as a priority one, the covered hours, when the clock pauses awaiting your input or a third party, and the remedy if we miss. Performance is reported from raw ticket data each month, not from a summary we assemble by hand.

A fixed monthly fee against a written scope, usually banded by supported users and devices, so the number is predictable and we have no reason to prefer slow fixes. What sits outside is named in the agreement rather than found in an invoice: hardware and third-party licence costs, project work such as an office move or a platform migration, cover beyond agreed hours, and any system you asked us to exclude. Scope changes are handled at review.

Plan for weeks, not a weekend. We begin with a discovery audit and rebuild the picture independently, covering tenants, devices, network kit, backups, licences and contracts, so the transition does not depend on a handover that may never arrive. Administrative credentials are reset into tenants you own and stored in a vault you control. Agent rollout and cutover run in waves, followed by hypercare. The same terms apply in reverse: if you leave us, the documentation and the keys are yours.

It is a fair challenge, and we keep the two apart. The team operating your estate is not the team that gives an independent opinion on it, and where you need assurance for a certification body, a customer or your board, we will say plainly that an assessor outside the delivery relationship carries more weight. What managed IT contributes is evidence: patch records, access reviews, joiner and leaver logs, backup restore tests and configuration baselines that any auditor can examine.
